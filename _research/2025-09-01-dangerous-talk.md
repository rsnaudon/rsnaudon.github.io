  **Abstract:** I study a continuous-time stopping game between two partners who gradually learn each other’s private type—normative or rogue—from exogenous public signals. A partner's type is irrelevant to joint output but determines incentives at termination: either partner may quit, normative partners may call out suspected rogues, and rogue partners may scheme with suspected accomplices. Stopping opportunities arrive privately, making the partnership’s survival an endogenous source of information. When opportunities are sufficiently infrequent, the game admits a unique symmetric Markov perfect equilibrium, in which normative partners are called out with positive probability. A one-sided learning benchmark links the costs of mistaken accusations to the ordering of quitting and call-out thresholds. In an intermediate regime, false accusations account for most dissolutions involving normative partners, while rogue partners are more likely to quit than to be exposed.
