  **Abstract:** A monopolist sells an indivisible good to consumers with unit demand who share a common valuation but differ in wealth. By selling a lottery that delivers the good only with some probability, the firm makes the good divisible in probability. When consumers can buy the same lottery repeatedly, it effectively works as a bundle whose size depends on how many draws each consumer can afford, screening consumers by wealth. Because willingness to pay is concave in the probability of delivery, a lottery can raise more revenue than a posted price. This repeated lottery is nevertheless dominated by a menu of one-shot lotteries. We show that selling a virtual currency that consumers then spend on lotteries lets the firm recover the menu's revenue, rationalizing a pricing layer common in loot-box markets.
